2. Did the quiz actually help? (the A/B test)¶

This notebook answers the real questions a decision-maker asks:

  1. How likely is it that the quiz is genuinely better?
  2. How big is the improvement, and how sure are we?
  3. What does it cost us if we're wrong?
  4. Are there any side effects we should worry about?
  5. Is it worth doing, and how should we roll it out?

We answer all five in everyday language, using a Bayesian approach. The statistics are in the cells, but you can follow the whole story from the text.

What does "Bayesian" mean here?¶

Imagine you're guessing how tall a stranger is. Before they stand up, you'd guess "about average". Then they stand next to a doorway and clearly are tall — so you update your guess. A Bayesian analysis is that idea, done with numbers: start with what you believe, then update it as the evidence comes in.

  • Prior — what we believed before seeing the experiment data.
  • Posterior — our updated belief after seeing it. This is the answer.
  • A credible interval is the range that most likely contains the true value.

We choose this approach because it speaks the language of decisions ("how likely is B better?", "what's the downside?") rather than the language of p-values.

Step 1 — What we believed beforehand (the prior)¶

Before this experiment we'd guess conversion is "somewhere around 40%" with a generous amount of uncertainty. We express that as a gentle bell-shaped belief. Being weak on purpose means the data, not our prior, drives the conclusion.

What this tells us. The two curves are well separated — they barely overlap. In plain terms: the data strongly points to B being the better version. If they overlapped a lot, we couldn't tell.

Step 3 — Turning beliefs into a decision¶

Now the numbers that a leader actually uses. In plain English:

  • P(B > A) — the chance B is genuinely better.
  • Likely lift — the size of the improvement, with a range.
  • Expected loss — the average downside if we pick the wrong one.

Step 5 — The health checks (guardrails)¶

A "win" that quietly hurts people isn't a win. So we track guardrail metrics — things that must not get worse. This is where the story gets interesting.

What this tells us — the twist. B nearly doubles the rate at which people change their fund later (from about 4% to 9%). That's a real cost to trust.

But look at the third bar: B actually makes people's fund choice fit their profile better. So the problem isn't a bad recommendation — it's speed. The quiz removes friction, so people commit before they've fully thought it through, then second-guess. The fix therefore isn't "improve the recommendation"; it's "add a moment of reassurance before confirming."

This is the bit a conversion-only analysis would miss entirely.

The recommendation¶

The numbers say B is a near-certain win worth millions. So why not launch it to everyone immediately? Because of the regret side effect — launching everywhere at once would lock in a real cost to trust that we haven't fixed yet.

So the recommendation is: roll it out to a quarter of people first, add a small reassurance step at the confirmation screen, watch the regret number, and then decide on going wider. Capture most of the upside while protecting the trust we've earned.

The one-sentence version for a leader: "The quiz lifts sign-ups by about 3 points and is almost certainly better, but it doubles the rate of second-guessing — so I'd stage the launch and fix that first, not ship it everywhere."

Honest limits¶

The data is synthetic, so this proves the method, not a real business result. It's New Zealand only, and the guardrail checks are measured on fewer people than the main number (so they're less precise). Full details in assumptions.md.
