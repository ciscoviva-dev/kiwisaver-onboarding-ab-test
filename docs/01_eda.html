1. First look at the data¶

Before running any statistics, a good analyst just looks at the data. This notebook does that: who the users are, where they drop off in the sign-up journey, and whether our experiment split people fairly.

Plain-English note. The data here is synthetic — made up by a simulator in this repo so we can test the method against a known answer. No real customer data is used. But it is designed to behave like the real KiwiSaver sign-up flow.

How to read this notebook¶

  • Cells with code produce the numbers and pictures.
  • The text between them explains, in everyday language, what we're seeing and why it matters. No prior stats knowledge needed.

2. The sign-up journey (the "funnel")¶

Think of the sign-up journey as a funnel: lots of people enter at the top, and each step loses some. The next chart shows how many people made it to each step, for each of the two versions of the app (A = the original fund list, B = the new quiz).

What this tells us. The biggest drop is before anyone even reaches the fund-selection step — most people who open the KiwiSaver page don't start the questionnaire. That's important: our experiment only changes the fund-picking step, so we deliberately measure success from the point people reach that step (the fair "starting line" for both versions). Otherwise the earlier drop-off — which is identical in both groups — would just add noise.

4. Did our random split work?¶

We flipped a coin to decide who saw A and who saw B. If that worked, the two groups should be a similar mix of people — the same only difference being the screen they saw. This is called a balance check, and the related tripwire is called SRM (sample-ratio mismatch).

In [4]:
split = users.variant.value_counts(normalize=True).round(3)
print("Share of users by arm:"); print(split)

for col in ["device", "channel", "is_existing_kiwisaver", "age_band"]:
    share = users.groupby(["variant", col]).size().unstack(0)
    share = (share / share.sum()).round(2)
    print(f"\n{col}:"); display(share)

What this tells us. The split is very close to 50/50, and each group has a similar mix of devices, channels and ages. That's exactly what we want: it means any difference in results later can be credited to the change, not to one group happening to have keener people in it.
